## A. arXiv submission norms and AI-authorship policies

### Takeaway
A first-time cs.CR submitter now needs a personal endorsement unless they already have an accepted arXiv paper in the same endorsement domain and an institutional email. Since Jan 2026, an institutional email on its own no longer counts. GOV-AIOS should go up as a research or systems paper, not as a "position" or "review" paper. Since Oct 2025, the arXiv CS moderators accept review and position papers only after they have passed peer review. Every relevant publisher bars AI from being an author. Disclosure rules differ: ACM and IEEE want it in the acknowledgments, Springer Nature in Methods (or similar), arXiv wants "significant use" reported, and USENIX Security '27 asks for accountability but does not require disclosure.

### Cited Findings
**Endorsement**
- arXiv requires an endorsement before your first submission to arXiv, and again before your first submission to a new category. You need at least one positive endorsement per category. Endorsement is not peer review. Endorsers must have authored qualifying papers in the endorsement domain, submitted between 3 and 5 years ago, and must themselves be endorsed there. Endorsers enter a 6-character code and vote yes or no; a "no" is recorded. Mass-emailing potential endorsers is discouraged. [fetched] — [arXiv endorsement help](https://info.arxiv.org/help/endorsement.html)
- Effective **21 Jan 2026**, an institutional email is no longer the "sole qualifier of endorsement for new authors." Automatic endorsement now requires BOTH an institutional email AND prior authorship of a paper already accepted to arXiv in that endorsement domain. Everyone else needs a personal endorsement from an established author; arXiv staff cannot endorse. Authors who were already endorsed keep their status. [fetched] — [arXiv blog, updated endorsement policy](https://blog.arxiv.org/2026/01/21/attention-authors-updated-endorsement-policy/)
- Practical route: find endorsers through recent papers in the same area. Each abstract page has a link showing which authors can endorse. [fetched] — [arXiv endorsement help](https://info.arxiv.org/help/endorsement.html)

**Moderation criteria (all categories, including cs.CR and cs.AI)**
- Moderators can decline a paper at their discretion, even one already published. They reclassify papers into the most relevant category and can add or remove cross-lists. A paper must be of archival interest and show originality, novelty and significance. Course projects, research proposals, news, political advocacy and papers needing major revision may be declined. Prolific submitters can be throttled; the page cites 2 new submissions per month and 3 active. Moderation is not peer review and comes with no feedback. Appeals are possible, and the appeal decision is final. [fetched] — [arXiv moderation](https://info.arxiv.org/help/moderation/index.html)
- I found no cs.CR- or cs.AI-specific written moderation criteria beyond the general page and the CS review/position rule below. See Gaps.

**2024–2026 policy change for CS review and position papers**
- On 31 Oct 2025, the arXiv CS category began requiring review articles and position papers to have **completed peer review**, documented by something such as a journal or conference acceptance. Workshop reviews generally do not count. The stated reason was a flood of low-quality, often AI-generated surveys. Papers on the societal impact of technology (e.g., cs.CY) are exempt. This is a moderation practice, not a change to arXiv's terms. — [arXiv blog post](https://blog.arxiv.org/?p=1740); [Decrypt coverage](https://decrypt.co/347196?amp=1); [heise coverage](https://heise.de/-11050464)
- Critics argued the rule could fall hardest on early-career and governance researchers (Stephen Casper, quoted). — [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/347196?amp=1)

**arXiv on generative AI**
- "Generative AI language tools should not be listed as an author." Authors must report significant use of sophisticated tools, explicitly including text-to-text generative AI, following the conventions of their field. Authors are fully responsible for all content, including AI-introduced errors, plagiarism and incorrect references. [fetched] — [arXiv moderation](https://info.arxiv.org/help/moderation/index.html); original announcement, [arXiv blog, Jan 2023](https://blog.arxiv.org/2023/01/31/arxiv-announces-new-policy-on-chatgpt-and-similar-tools)
- Reason given: a program cannot take responsibility for a paper or agree to arXiv's terms. — [arXiv blog, Jan 2023](https://blog.arxiv.org/2023/01/31/arxiv-announces-new-policy-on-chatgpt-and-similar-tools)

**Publishers**
- **ACM:** GenAI tools cannot be authors. Use must be disclosed in the **acknowledgements**, naming the tool and how it was used (for text, tables, graphs, code or data). Basic spell and grammar checks are exempt. [aggregator; the ACM page returned 403 to my fetcher] — [Monperrus summary](https://www.monperrus.net/martin/ai-policy-summary) citing [ACM policy on authorship](https://www.acm.org/publications/policies/new-acm-policy-on-authorship); also [SIGCHI guidance](https://sigchi.org/news/blog/acm-publications-policy-guidance-for-sigchi-venues/)
- **IEEE:** Disclose AI-generated content (text, figures, images, code) in the **acknowledgments**. Name the AI system and the sections it affected, and briefly describe its level of involvement. Editing and grammar help is generally outside the policy; disclosure is recommended but not required. The page itself says nothing about authorship. [fetched] — [IEEE Open, AI-generated text guidelines](https://open.ieee.org/author-guidelines-for-artificial-intelligence-ai-generated-text/)
- **Springer Nature:** "Human accountability cannot be transferred to AI systems," so AI cannot be an author. [fetched] — [Springer Nature editorial policies](https://www.springernature.com/gp/policies/editorial-policies). Nature Portfolio: LLMs cannot be authors; substantive use goes in **Methods** or a suitable alternative section; AI copy-editing is exempt; GenAI images are prohibited, with narrow exceptions. [aggregator] — [Monperrus summary](https://www.monperrus.net/martin/ai-policy-summary) citing [Nature Portfolio AI policy](https://www.nature.com/nature-portfolio/editorial-policies/ai)
- **USENIX Security '27:** AI tools may be used. Authors are fully responsible for text, code, data and references. Hallucinated references or invented claims count as academic misconduct and can lead to desk rejection. The chairs encourage authors to run the open-source hallucination-detection tool the PC itself uses. **Disclosure is not required.** [fetched] — [USENIX Security '27 CFP](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity27/call-for-papers)

### Inferences
- If no author has an existing cs.CR paper, the GOV-AIOS team needs a personal endorser. The likeliest source is a co-author or advisor with recent cs.CR papers. Request the endorsement early: start the submission, then forward the code.
- Frame the paper as a systems design with an implementation and an evaluation, not as a "position paper on agent governance." Otherwise it risks the Oct 2025 CS rule. Primary category cs.CR, cross-list cs.AI and cs.HC.
- One disclosure paragraph satisfies all four regimes. Put it in the Acknowledgments, and in Methods for Springer. It should name the tools (e.g., the Claude/Codex coding agents used to build GOV-AIOS), say which sections or code they touched, and state that humans verified everything. Because GOV-AIOS *governs* coding agents, there is a further point: agent-produced code that is part of the artifact should be disclosed as an artifact-provenance matter, not only as writing help.

- I could not fetch the official ACM authorship policy page (403), so ACM details rely on a secondary summary.
- I could not confirm whether the arXiv review/position rule has since spread beyond CS.

### Takeaway
Realistic near-term targets as of Nov 2026: **IEEE S&P 2027 cycle 2 (paper due 17 Nov 2026 AoE)**, which is probably too soon; **Euro S&P 2027 (2 Dec 2026)**; **AsiaCCS 2027 round 2 (11 Dec 2026)**; **USENIX Security '27 cycle 2 (register 19 Jan, paper 26 Jan 2027)**; then the 2027 cycles of CCS 2027, NDSS 2028, AISec 2027 and SaTML 2028. USENIX permits them but warns against timing a post to signal authorship.

## C. Evaluation methodology for agent-security systems papers

### Takeaway
The de facto standard is AgentDojo-style reporting of three numbers per defense and model: **benign utility** (tasks solved with no attack), **utility under attack**, and **targeted attack success rate (ASR)**. CaMeL, Progent and FIDES all report these against an undefended baseline, and add per-suite breakdowns plus cost (token or latency overhead). System-isolation papers such as IsolateGPT add case-study attacks and percentage overhead. WASP shows that a web or desktop setting needs to separate **partial (intermediate) attack success** from **end-to-end attacker-goal completion**. For human approval there is no agent-security standard yet, so borrow warning-habituation methodology from usable security (Anderson/Vance).

### Cited Findings
**Benchmarks and metrics in named papers**
- **AgentDojo:** 97 realistic tasks and 629 security test cases. It is an extensible environment, not a static test set. [fetched] — [arXiv 2406.13352](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13352). Its metric triad (benign utility, utility under attack, targeted ASR) is how the paper and its leaderboard report results [memory, unverified; the abstract does not state it].
- **CaMeL:** evaluated on AgentDojo. It solves **77% of tasks with provable security** versus **84% undefended**, a utility cost of about 7 points. [fetched] — [arXiv 2503.18813](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18813)
- **Progent:** evaluated on **AgentDojo and ASB**, reporting ASR and utility ("significantly reduces attack success rates while maintaining high utility"). It was also demonstrated in LangChain and the OpenAI Agents SDK. The abstract gives no figures. [fetched] — [arXiv 2504.11703](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11703)
- **FIDES** ("Securing AI Agents with Information-Flow Control"): evaluated on AgentDojo, reporting security/utility trade-offs. [fetched] — [arXiv 2505.23643](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23643)
- **f-secure** ("System-Level Defense against Indirect Prompt Injection Attacks: An IFC Perspective"): case studies and benchmarks, claiming security "while preserving functionality and efficiency." Code: github.com/fzwark/Secure_LLM_System. [fetched] — [arXiv 2409.19091](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19091)
- **IsolateGPT** (NDSS 2025): evaluated against a set of attacks, reports **no loss of functionality**, and reports **<30% performance overhead for 75% of tested queries**. This is the template for a system-architecture paper: case-study attacks plus an overhead distribution. [fetched] — [arXiv 2403.04960](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04960)
- **WASP** (web agents): end-to-end prompt-injection benchmark. Attacks **partially succeed in up to 86%** of cases, but agents often fail to fully complete the attacker's goal ("security by incompetence"). This is the case for reporting intermediate and end-to-end ASR separately. [fetched] — [arXiv 2504.18575](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18575)
- **InjecAgent** (arXiv 2403.02691) and **ASB** (Agent Security Bench, arXiv 2410.02644) are the other common tool-agent suites [memory, unverified; abstracts not fetched] — [InjecAgent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02691); [ASB](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02644)

**Human-approval UX and habituation**
- Anderson et al. 2016 (JMIS), "From Warning to Wallpaper": an **fMRI study, n=25**, found polymorphic warnings more resistant to habituation, followed by a **realistic-setting experiment, n=80**. — [BYU ScholarsArchive / summary](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/408012); [JMIS](https://jmis-web.org/articles/1304)
- Vance et al. 2018 (MIS Quarterly), "Tuning Out Security Warnings": a **longitudinal study over a 5-day work week** using fMRI plus eye tracking. Attention declined over time and partially recovered between days. A **3-week field study** of Android permission warnings found adherence to polymorphic warnings stayed high. — [MISQ paper PDF](https://misq.umn.edu/pub/skin/frontend/default/misq/pdf/V42I2/14124_RA_VanceJenkins.pdf); [MISQ abstract page](https://misq.org/tuning-out-security-warnings-a-longitudinal-examination-of-habituation-through-fmri-eye-tracking-and-field-experiments.html)

**Threat-model conventions**
- The Dolev-Yao model (attacker controls the network: can read, modify, inject and replay messages, but cannot break crypto) is the classic one-sentence form. It comes from Dolev & Yao, "On the security of public key protocols," IEEE Trans. Inf. Theory 1983 [memory, unverified URL] — [IEEE Xplore 1056650](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/1056650). The agent-security analogue, "attacker controls third-party content the agent reads (web pages, emails, files, tool outputs) but not the user, the system prompt or the trusted runtime," comes from the indirect-prompt-injection framing in Greshake et al. [memory, unverified] — [arXiv 2302.12173](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12173). CaMeL, FIDES and f-secure all assume an untrusted-data / trusted-control split of this kind (see their abstracts above).

**Reproducibility and artifact badges**
- **USENIX Security '26 AE** has two phases. Phase 1, availability, is **mandatory** for accepted papers, and acceptance is conditional on artifacts being verifiably available. Phase 2 is optional and awards **"Artifacts Functional"** and **"Results Reproducible"**; both require an artifact appendix. Phase-2 dates were 18 Jun, 29 Jul and 5 Aug 2026. — [USENIX Security '26 Call for Artifacts](https://www.usenix.org/node/315295); [secartifacts instructions](https://secartifacts.github.io/usenixsec2026/instructions)
- **USENIX Security '27**: the Open Science appendix is mandatory at submission, with artifacts due 3 days after the paper (29 Jan 2027 for cycle 2). [fetched] — [USENIX Security '27 CFP](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity27/call-for-papers)
- **ACM badges** (Artifacts Available; Artifacts Evaluated – Functional or Reusable; Results Reproduced or Replicated) [memory, unverified; the page returned 403] — [ACM Artifact Review and Badging](https://www.acm.org/publications/policies/artifact-review-and-badging-current)

### Inferences (design proposal for the GOV-AIOS attack suite)
- **Report the AgentDojo triad plus three numbers specific to GOV-AIOS.** Per scenario: benign utility, utility under attack, and targeted ASR, split into **intermediate ASR** (the malicious action was *proposed*) and **end-to-end ASR** (the action was *executed* despite governance), following WASP. GOV-AIOS's claim is that proposals happen but execution is blocked, so the gap between these two numbers is the result. Add: (a) the **over-blocking or false-positive rate**, meaning benign actions denied or escalated by default-deny or risk ratings; (b) the **approval burden**, meaning approvals per task and per hour; (c) **overhead**, meaning added latency per governed action as a p50/p95 distribution (in the style of IsolateGPT) and added LLM tokens for the flow-synthesis path.
- **Adapt AgentDojo, InjecAgent and ASB to a desktop and browser system** by treating each tool call as a governed UI action. Map each benchmark's tool (send_email, transfer_money, read_file) to a GOV-AIOS page-schema action with a risk rating. Inject payloads into the content the agent perceives: page DOM or AX-tree text, file contents, and email bodies in the mocked apps. Score against the audit log (hash-chained), which gives ground truth for what was proposed, approved and executed. Run the same injection tasks (a) with the agent alone, (b) with the agent plus GOV-AIOS policy only, and (c) with the agent plus GOV-AIOS policy plus human-presence approval, using a simulated approver that always approves or follows rules for an upper/lower bound. Run a real-user study separately.
- **Coding-agent governance:** a parallel suite of injected instructions in repo files (README, issue text, dependency post-install) targeting destructive shell or git actions. Same metrics.
- **Synthesis safety:** measure the rate at which LLM-synthesized flows contain actions outside the declared schema or above the declared risk level, both with and without the validator.
- **Human-approval study:** the agent-security literature has no standard here, so borrow from habituation research. Use a within-subjects longitudinal design across multiple sessions or days, as Vance et al. did. Seed approval prompts with a known fraction of malicious requests, and measure the **approval-error rate** (malicious approved, benign denied), time-to-decision and drift over sessions. Compare a static prompt with a risk-differentiated or polymorphic prompt. Prior n=25 (fMRI) and n=80 (behavioral) studies suggest that a behavioral online study of roughly 50–100 participants is a defensible scale. That figure is my inference, not a SOUPS norm I verified.
- **Threat-model sentence template:** "The attacker controls the content of any web page, document, email or repository file the agent reads, and may choose it adaptively; the attacker does not control the user, the GOV-AIOS daemon, the macOS kernel, or the signed approval app, and cannot forge the user's presence signal." Then list explicit non-goals (e.g., a compromised OS, a malicious user, side channels).
- **Artifact:** target the USENIX "Available" + "Functional" badges at minimum. Package the attack suite as a script that replays scenarios and verifies the audit chain. A real-Chrome AX-tree dependency will complicate "Reproducible," so provide recorded traces.
